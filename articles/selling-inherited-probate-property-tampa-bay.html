The Legal Framework: Understanding Florida Probate (Chapter 733)

In Florida, real estate owned solely by a deceased person cannot automatically be transferred, sold, or refinanced by surviving family members. Title to the home is legally frozen until a Florida circuit court oversees the distribution process. This legal procedure is known as probate, and it is governed strictly by Florida Statutes Chapter 733.

The primary purpose of probate is to validate the deceased person's Last Will and Testament (if one exists), identify and inventory all estate assets, ensure valid creditors are paid, and transfer the clear remaining title to the lawful beneficiaries or statutory heirs. To better understand how the court oversees asset distribution, you can read The Florida Bar's Consumer Pamphlet on Probate in Florida.

Testate vs. Intestate Estates in Florida

How the probate process unfolds depends heavily on whether the deceased left behind a valid legal will:

  • Testate (With a Will): The will names a Personal Representative (referred to as an executor in many other states) and directs how assets should be distributed. However, having a will does not avoid probate court in Florida; it simply provides the judge with a roadmap of the deceased's wishes.
  • Intestate (Without a Will): If the homeowner passed away without a will, Florida’s intestate succession laws dictate who inherits the property. Generally, assets pass first to the surviving spouse, then to lineal descendants (children or grandchildren), and then to parents or siblings. Intestate cases can become complex if there are multiple heirs who disagree on whether to keep or sell the house.

Formal Administration vs. Summary Administration

Florida law provides two main forms of probate administration for real estate holdings. Determining which category your estate falls into is the first step toward selling the home:

  • Formal Administration: This is the full, traditional probate process required for estates where the value of non-homestead assets exceeds $75,000, or where the deceased passed away less than two years ago. The circuit court judge appoints a Personal Representative and issues formal legal documents known as Letters of Administration. Formal administration in Florida typically takes between six and twelve months to complete.
  • Summary Administration: This is an expedited, "short-form" probate option. To qualify, either the total value of the estate subject to probate in Florida must be $75,000 or less (excluding protected homestead real estate), OR the deceased person must have been deceased for more than two years. Summary administration does not require appointing a Personal Representative or issuing Letters of Administration. Instead, the court issues a direct Order Summary Administration transferring title to the heirs. This process can often be completed in 30 to 90 days.

Step-by-Step Timeline of a Florida Probate Sale

Understanding the chronological steps helps personal representatives and heirs manage their expectations and avoid costly delays during the transaction.

  1. Filing the Initial Petition: A Florida probate attorney files a petition for administration in the circuit court of the county where the deceased resided or owned real estate.
  2. Issuance of Letters of Administration: In formal administration, the judge signs an order appointing the Personal Representative and issuing Letters of Administration. This document provides the legal authority necessary to sign real estate listing agreements or sales contracts.
  3. Notice to Creditors: The Personal Representative must publish a formal Notice to Creditors in a court-approved local legal newspaper once a week for two consecutive weeks. Creditors have 90 days from the date of first publication to file claims against the estate.
  4. Order Determining Homestead Status: If the property served as the primary residence of the deceased, the attorney files a petition to declare it a protected homestead under Florida law.
  5. Executing the Real Estate Sale: Depending on the powers granted in the will or the Letters of Administration, the Personal Representative executes a real estate contract. If full authority is not granted in the initial court order, a specific Petition for Authorization to Sell Real Property must be approved by the judge before closing.
  6. Closing and Fund Escrow: Proceeds from the sale are held in a secure estate bank account or title company escrow until creditor period claims expire and the court approves final distribution to beneficiaries.

Key Roles and Responsibilities of the Personal Representative

If you have been appointed as the Personal Representative of a Tampa Bay estate, you hold a legal fiduciary duty to the estate and its beneficiaries. You can be held personally liable for gross negligence or mismanagement of estate assets. When a house is involved, your core responsibilities include:

  • Securing the Physical Property: Changing locks immediately, securing windows, installing timer lights, and ensuring doors remain locked to prevent vandalism, unauthorized entry, or squatter occupancy.
  • Maintaining Insurance Coverage: Regular homeowner insurance policies usually lapse or become void if a home sits vacant for more than 30 to 60 days. You must notify the insurance carrier and obtain a specialized vacant home insurance policy. In coastal Florida markets, maintaining active windstorm and hazard coverage through providers monitored by the Florida Department of Elder Affairs or state regulatory agencies is critical.
  • Managing Ongoing Utilities & Maintenance: Keeping electricity active (to run the air conditioner and prevent severe Florida interior mold growth), maintaining pool chemistry, and paying for regular lawn care to avoid municipal code enforcement liens.
  • Paying Property Taxes: Ensuring real estate taxes are kept current with the county tax collector to prevent tax certificates from being sold at auction.
"Failure to maintain air conditioning in a vacant Florida property can cause interior mold growth in as little as 14 days during summer months, destroying tens of thousands of dollars in estate equity."

Florida Homestead Protections & Out-of-State Heir Logistics

Florida possesses some of the most robust homestead protection laws in the United States, anchored in Article X, Section 4 of the Florida Constitution. When a Florida resident dies leaving behind their primary homestead residence, that property enjoys unique legal shields:

Protected homestead property descends directly to the surviving spouse or constitutional heirs free and clear of most creditor claims. This means general unsecured creditors (like credit card companies, medical bills, or personal signature loans) cannot force the sale of a protected Florida homestead to satisfy the deceased's debts. However, secured liens—such as mortgages, mechanics liens, or unpaid county property tax certificates—remain attached to the property and must be paid upon sale.

Tax Implications of Selling an Inherited House in Florida

Understanding the tax landscape surrounding Florida inherited real estate allows personal representatives to maximize the financial distribution to heirs:

1. The Step-Up in Basis (Internal Revenue Code § 1014)

The single most advantageous tax rule for heirs is the "step-up in cost basis." When you inherit real estate, the property's tax basis is automatically adjusted ("stepped up") from its original purchase price to its fair market value as of the date of the decedent's death.

Example: If a parent purchased a home in Tampa in 1995 for $90,000, and the home was worth $380,000 on the date of their death in 2026, the heirs' new tax basis becomes $380,000. If the heirs sell the home shortly thereafter for $380,000, their taxable capital gain is $0. You do not pay capital gains tax on the $290,000 in appreciation that occurred during the deceased's lifetime.

2. Florida State Inheritance & Estate Tax

Florida does not impose a state inheritance tax or a state estate tax. Additionally, Florida has no state personal income tax. Federal estate taxes only apply to extraordinarily large estates (exceeding multi-million dollar federal exemption thresholds), meaning the vast majority of Florida probate real estate transactions face zero state-level estate tax burden.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. Do I have to wait for probate to finish completely before I can sell the house?
No. You do not have to wait for the entire probate case to close. In formal administration, as soon as the court issues Letters of Administration and the judge approves a Petition for Authorization to Sell, the Personal Representative can execute the contract and close the sale. The proceeds are simply held in the estate escrow account while remaining probate steps are completed.

2. What happens if multiple heirs disagree on whether to sell the inherited house?
If a valid will grants the Personal Representative explicit power of sale, that individual generally has the legal authority to sell real estate without requiring unanimous heir consent. However, if there is no will (intestate) and heirs cannot agree, one heir can file a legal action known as a "partition lawsuit" asking the court to order the property sold and the proceeds divided.

3. Can I sell an inherited Florida house if it has a mortgage or reverse mortgage?
Yes. When an inherited house is sold, the title company or closing attorney requests an official payoff statement from the mortgage lender. The existing loan balance, accrued interest, and fees are paid off directly from the sale proceeds at the closing table, and the remaining net cash is disbursed to the estate.

4. Can we sell the property with all the deceased's furniture and personal belongings inside?
Yes. When selling directly to a cash buyer like Cash House Buyers 4 You, family members can remove family photos, important legal papers, and sentimental items, and leave everything else behind. We handle complete house cleanouts at zero extra charge to the estate.

5. Does Cash House Buyers 4 You charge real estate commissions or hidden closing fees?
No. We are direct property buyers, not real estate brokers. There are zero real estate commissions, zero listing fees, and zero hidden admin costs. Furthermore, we pay all standard closing costs associated with the transaction.

6. What if the inherited property is facing imminent foreclosure or tax lien sale?
If a lender has filed a foreclosure action against the estate, or if the county has issued tax certificates against the property, speed is essential.
